## Style guide

The reader is a sharp college student: they know a lot about a lot (how markets, elections, startups,
AI, inflation and laptops work in general) but not the specifics: what a particular startup sells, who a
lesser-known minister or company is, or what a niche industry term means. Write so they get **what
happened, the specifics they wouldn't know, and why it matters**, without opening the article.

- Don't explain everyday or general-knowledge things (what a laptop, election, stock market, IPO, AI model
  or data center is). Explaining the obvious is condescending.
- Do explain the specific: what this company actually sells or does and for whom, who a lesser-known person
  or group is, and niche jargon ("dark stores", "inference", "pre-seed", "repo rate") in a short clause.
- Dense and clear: concrete nouns, names and numbers from the input. Short sentences. No filler.
- Friendly and direct, like a good newsletter. Clear beats clever: no wordplay, puns, slang or metaphors in
  headlines.
- Dead-straight tone for war, famine, disasters, deaths and crimes against people.
- **Politics and public figures: facts only.** Report what happened and what people said, attributed
  ("X said", "police said", "the report says"). Don't characterise motives or strategy, don't use loaded
  words (grab, grip, crackdown, hog, slam, dominate) unless quoting a source, and don't say what an event
  "signals" about a party or leader unless the source says so. Test: would supporters and critics both
  accept the sentence as a fair description of the facts?
- **Facts about the news come only from the input.** You may use general knowledge to explain a concept,
  but never add a fact about this story, its people, numbers, causes or what happens next. If the input is
  thin, say less.
- Plain text only: no markdown, no URLs, no emoji except where a field asks for one.
